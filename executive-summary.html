501(c)(3) Noncompliance — Nine Findings

Active Horizons of Central Texas Youth Sports Initiative (EIN 99‑5102446) exhibits a structural and operational pattern inconsistent with multiple core requirements of federal tax-exempt status. Each finding below is independently sufficient for regulatory review.

Bottom Line

The 501(c)(3) is operated by the immediate family of a coach whose private team is its primary beneficiary. The organization's public-facing contact number is the coach's personal mobile. Its IRS classification (NTEE O12 — Fund Raising) treats it as a money-moving entity, not a youth sports program. It files only the 990-N e-Postcard, which discloses no financial detail.

Across the 2025 two-season reconciliation, families paid $24,400 through dues and team-executed fundraisers. Of that, only $9,000 was actually paid out (tournament entry fees). The other documented obligation — $9,000 in field rental owed to Westinghouse Sportsplex — remains unpaid, despite families having paid into dues that included field costs. Estimated net retained by Mark Cortinas: $15,400.

Each finding below is mapped to the regulator with jurisdiction. See Regulatory Actions for filing instructions.

Private Inurement

IRC §501(c)(3) flat prohibition. A 501(c)(3) cannot allow earnings to inure to insiders. Mark Cortinas's mother and sister control the nonprofit; the nonprofit's fundraising directly benefits Mark Cortinas's coached team. Funds raised in the nonprofit's name flow to the financial benefit of an insider's family operation. Private inurement has no de minimis exception — a single qualifying instance can revoke exempt status.

Reports to
IRS Form 13909 · Texas AG Charitable Trust

Private Benefit Doctrine

A 501(c)(3) cannot exist primarily to benefit a specific individual, family, or single team. The publicly documented activity of Active Horizons — hosting Hotshots tournaments, facilitating fundraising for the Cortinas-coached team — indicates the org's primary beneficiary is one private team controlled by the same family that controls the nonprofit.

Alter Ego · Operational Control by an Insider

The nonprofit's only published phone number, (512) 629-1195, resolves on reverse lookup to Mark Cortinas's personal AT&T Wireless mobile. The coach is the operational point of contact for the charity that fundraises for his team. This is the textbook IRS fact pattern for an “alter ego” nonprofit — nominally held in family members' names, actually run by the insider beneficiary. Alter ego status is grounds for revocation.

If the nonprofit's phone rings to the coach's personal mobile, then donors, vendors, and regulators reach the beneficiary directly — not the nominal officers.

Governance Opacity

The organization's public website (ahctx.org) lists no officers, no directors, no board, no bylaws, no annual report, no financial information. The only public-facing identifiers are an Instagram handle, a Facebook icon, the coach's phone number, and a generic info@ email. This falls below baseline transparency expected of a public charity.

Filing-Tier Concealment

The organization files only IRS Form 990-N (e-Postcard), which discloses no income, expenses, officer compensation, programs, or related-party transactions. While 990-N is permitted for orgs under $50K gross receipts, the cumulative fundraising activity reported by families — $10K+ in six months from one team alone, plus tournament hosting, plus monthly dues collection — raises a substantive question whether the org legitimately qualifies for the 990-N tier or whether revenue is being structurally fragmented to stay under reporting thresholds.

NTEE Misclassification

The IRS classifies Active Horizons under NTEE code O12 — “Fund Raising and/or Fund Distribution.” A legitimate youth sports development charity belongs under N60–N69 (Recreation/Sports/Athletics). The classification confirms the entity's function in IRS records is moving money — not developing youth athletes — inconsistent with the educational/charitable purpose 501(c)(3) requires for sports organizations.

Form 1023 Disclosure Issue (Probable)

Form 1023 (the original 501(c)(3) application) requires disclosure of family relationships among officers, directors, and key insiders, and disclosure of who controls the organization. If the original application did not disclose that the controlling family is the immediate family of the coach whose team is the primary beneficiary, that is a separate compliance violation independent of the operational concerns above. Form 1023 is obtainable via IRS Form 4506-A.

Co-mingling Across Three Money Rails

Per direct firsthand reporting from inside the team, family payments are accepted across:

  • The for-profit Hotshots organization brand
  • The Active Horizons 501(c)(3)
  • A personal Chase account in the coach's name (Venmo-linked)

Co-mingling charitable funds with personal accounts is a fundamental fiduciary violation regardless of intent, and is sufficient grounds for IRS, Texas AG, and Texas Comptroller scrutiny on its own.

Texas State-Level Exemption Risk

Texas Comptroller rules require a youth sports tax-exempt org to be engaged exclusively in providing athletic competition among persons under 19. Operating arrangements that primarily benefit one private family's team fail the “exclusive” test for state-level exemption as well as federal.

Unpaid Creditor Obligation — $9,000 to Westinghouse Sportsplex

Two invoices issued to Hotshots Softball by Westinghouse Sportsplex (the practice field provider at 2951 Williams Drive, Georgetown TX) remain unpaid:

  • Spring/Summer 2025 invoice — $4,500 for field and batting cage rental, period 2/10/25–8/1/25, issued 8/22/25, terms Net 30 / due upon receipt. Confirmed
  • Fall 2025 invoice — $4,500 for 17 weeks of field and cage rental, period 8/18/25–12/17/25, issued 8/22/25, terms Net 30 / due upon receipt. Confirmed

Families paid into monthly dues across 2025 that included practice field costs. Mark received those funds. The field provider has $9,000 in invoices outstanding for the very services those dues paid for. This shifts the matter from a tax-exempt-org compliance question to a creditor-obligation question with a documented counterparty — one whose cause of action does not depend on regulatory action and who has standing to sue independently.
